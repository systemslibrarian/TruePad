How online pad delivery works

A receive code gives the sender the public information needed to seal one pad for you. Your private key stays in TruePad. After you add the pad, normal messages use the one-time pad as usual.

Online cryptography delivers the pad. The one-time pad protects the messages.
The delivery layer is computational — it depends on certain mathematical problems being hard. A pad you carry by hand does not. That difference is what this page is about.

A one-time locked mailbox

Creating a receive code is like putting out a one-time locked mailbox. The code is the part anyone may see: it carries what a sender needs in order to lock something for that mailbox. The private half needed to open it never leaves your browser.

There is no mailbox on a server. TruePad has no backend and no account — nothing is deposited anywhere, and no TruePad service ever sees your code, your sealed file, or your pad.

1. The recipient creates a receive code

TruePad generates a fresh one-time recipient keypair. The public half goes into the receive code; the private half stays inside your TruePad and is never copied into it.

The code is one line of text beginning with TPR2:. It is not secret. Send it by chat, email, or text. Someone who copies it cannot open anything with it — it is the lock, not the key.

2. They compare 12 words

If someone replaced the recipient's code with their own, the sender could seal the pad for the wrong person without noticing. So TruePad turns the exact receive request into twelve words. Both people see twelve words, and compare all twelve, in order.

Matching words are evidence of one specific thing: both people are looking at the same receive code. They are not proof of anyone's identity — that comes from the channel you compare them over, such as a voice you recognise.

3. The sender seals the pad

TruePad uses the public material in the receive code to make a fresh encryption key that only the recipient's saved private key can recover, and encrypts the existing pad into a sealed .tps2 file. That file is a sealed copy of the pad — it is not the raw pad file.

4. The sender sends the sealed file

The sealed file can travel through an ordinary channel because it is encrypted transport rather than the pad itself. TruePad does not send it — it makes the file, and you choose the channel. Nothing is uploaded.

What it containsOrdinary channel?
Raw .pad the secret pad itself No. Anyone holding it can read and forge this pad's messages.
Sealed .tps2 an encrypted delivery of the pad, locked to one receive request Yes — that is what it is for.

5. The recipient opens the sealed pad

TruePad finds the private key it saved with that receive request, checks the package is bound to that same request, decrypts the pad, and keeps it inside its worker while the confirmation ceremony is still going on. Nothing is saved until the recipient accepts.

6. They compare 8 confirmation words

The twelve words checked the receive request before the pad was sent. The eight check the sealed package afterwards.

The recipient reads his eight words first. Only after the sender says she has heard them does TruePad show her own — before that they are not on her screen at all. Hearing his first hands an impersonator a value they must already have matched, rather than a target to repeat back.

TruePad controls the order of its own screens. It cannot know whether two people actually spoke in that order, or compared carefully. That part is up to you.

7. Then normal messaging begins

Once the pad is added, the delivery cryptography is finished and never runs again. X-Wing, ML-KEM and the sealed file's AES-GCM are not used for messages.

DELIVERY: X-Wing (ML-KEM-768 + X25519) → HKDF-SHA-256 → AES-256-GCM

MESSAGES: one-time pad + Wegman–Carter authentication

Why online delivery is a different security claim

Hand the pad over privately and, if the one-time pad's assumptions hold, the delivery fits the conditional information-theoretic model the OTP is known for. Nothing about it depends on a computation being hard.

Online delivery is different: the pad is wrapped in ordinary modern cryptography first, and someone watching the channel can keep the sealed file indefinitely. Today that archived copy should be useless to them. It could stop being useless if the delivery cryptography is broken in future, or if the recipient's saved one-time key state is restored or cloned from a backup. Either would reveal the pad — and the pad decrypts the messages made with it.

Deleting your local copy of a sealed file does not erase copies already sent through chat, email, cloud storage, or backups. TruePad cannot reach those, and cannot prove any copy has been physically erased.

Common questions

Is the receive code secret?

No. It carries public information only; the private key does not appear in it.

Can I email the sealed .tps2?

Yes, if that is the channel you choose. It is encrypted transport locked to one request.

Can I email the raw .pad?

No. The raw pad is the secret itself. Hand it over in person, or use a channel only the two of you control.

Does X-Wing encrypt every message?

No. It delivers the pad once. Messages then use the one-time pad and Wegman–Carter authentication.

Are the words passwords?

No. They are a readable form of a cryptographic value, meant to be spoken and compared. There is nowhere to type them.

Does matching the words prove who someone is?

No. It proves you are both looking at the same values. Who you are talking to comes from the channel you compare them over.

Technical details
Receive requestTPR2: + canonical unpadded base64url, 1652 characters
KEMX-Wing, draft-connolly-cfrg-xwing-kem-10, suite 0x0001 — ML-KEM-768 with X25519
Key derivationHKDF-SHA-256
Pad transport encryptionAES-256-GCM
Sealed packageTPS2, saved as .tps2
Request comparison12 words / 132 bits
Package confirmation8 words / 88 bits
After importthe existing TruePad one-time pad + one-time Wegman–Carter authentication

ML-KEM is standardised (NIST FIPS 203). X-Wing is a draft — TruePad has frozen the draft-10 construction. It is not a NIST standard, not an RFC, and not IETF-approved. The delivery claim is computational.

The words come from the BIP-39 English list, used purely as a readable mapping for fixed 11-bit indices. They are not wallet mnemonics and not a recovery phrase — nothing is derived from them and they unlock nothing.

A page that already holds worker authority is endpoint compromise, and no ordering in these ceremonies changes that. All browser state is one rollback domain: restoring a whole profile can rewind counters, markers and request state together.
